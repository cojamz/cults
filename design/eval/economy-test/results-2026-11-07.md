# Economy test: results (Nov 7)

Nothing in the live game changed. Today's price: every member costs its printed price + 1 of its color + 2 any (grey + 3 any).

## Bottom line
- Every way of making members pricier gives the same trade: **fewer cards to track, recruits feel planned, but more turns where you can't afford any member**. Judges felt the first two clearly and the third clearly.
- The one mix that kept the game feeling big while cutting cards: **members 1 more of their color and 1 more any, and every space gives 1 more of its color**. Judges preferred it to today 5 to 1.
- Some of the worst moments come from two cards, not from prices (end of page).

## Tier 1: try at the table
**Members +1 color +1 any, every space +1 of its color**
- At the table: members cost about 9 instead of 7, but each space pays about 1 more, so you still afford something most turns. Fewer cards come out, and people keep and use the ones they buy instead of selling them back.
- Judges: 5 of 6 preferred it to today on the same deal. Recruits felt like events (5 to 0), the race stayed close (4 to 1). It was the only pricier mix that didn't make the game feel smaller.
- Numbers (Hard bots, 150 games; Normal bots, 300 agree): 0.6 to 0.7 fewer member cards each, about 6 more turns in 100 where no member is affordable, final followers about +4.

## Tier 2: good, with one cost
**Members +1 of their color** (grey +1 any)
- At the table: the easiest table to read of anything tested. Fewer, sharper members, and no player won just by refusing members.
- Judges: 5 of 6 preferred it to today. Every judge said it was easier to follow (6 to 0). The cost: more dry turns (4 to 1 against) and a smaller-feeling game (5 to 0 against).
- Numbers: about 1 fewer member card each, about 10 more turns in 100 with no member affordable, the most of any tier 1 or 2 mix.

## Tier 3: split
**Follower buys pay a bonus: 5 for 6, 10 for 13** (1 for 1 unchanged)
- At the table: buying followers becomes a real rival to members, scores pack close at the end (6 to 0 judges), and it's the only change that cut cards and also cut dry turns.
- Judges split 3 to 3. Against: round III turns into "take a space, buy 13 for 10" over and over (choices 5 to 0 worse, size 4 to 0 worse).
- Numbers: 0.7 fewer cards, 4 fewer dry turns in 100, final followers +11 to +12.

**Every space gives 1 less of its color**
- Judges 2 to 1 for it, but the casual player clearly against: quiet early turns with nothing to buy. Final followers drop by 7.

## Tier 4: no
**Members +1 color +1 any with the follower bonus**
- Members stop mattering: 4 in 5 games are won by a player with 5 or fewer member cards, and judges preferred today 2 to 1.

## Dials that barely moved anything (numbers only, 60 games each)
- The round I deck limit (members up to 4 or up to 6 in round I): no change.
- How much of a member's price is its color versus any: small (0.4 fewer cards when 1 more is colored).
- Contest prizes (+3/+5/+8 or +6/+9/+14): no change in cards; bigger prizes widen the winning margin.
- Cloak's starting resources (3 instead of 1): slightly more cards.
- Spaces paying more in round I and less in round III: slightly fewer dry turns, slightly more cards. Not judged.

## Seen in every economy (cards, not prices)
- **Con Men** ("On Recruit: steal 5 resources."): swapped between cults by **Promote** and **Demote**, it steals 5 again on every move. Judges saw it change hands 4 to 7 times a game. The most-named worst moment.
- **Largesse** ("Give rivals 12 silver from the bank, split as you choose. +10 followers.") copied 3 to 4 times in round III through Green's copy spaces and **Fan Club**.
- Round III often ends in resource piles turned into the biggest follower buy, at every price tested.

## How it was tested
- 36 mixes of member price, its color part, space gains, cloak gains, cloak start, round I deck limit, follower buys and contest prizes. 60 Hard bot games each on the same deals; the 6 most different got 150 Hard and 300 Normal games. A change counts only when both kinds of bots agree and it's bigger than luck.
- Feel: whole games written out turn by turn. 3 judges (one reading as you, one seasoned worker-placement player, one casual friend) compared each mix with today on the same deal, without being told which was which: 24 comparisons over 2 to 3 deals each. A first round of 21 judges scoring each mix alone couldn't tell mixes apart (every mix about 3 of 5), so comparisons on the same deal decide.
- Judges were checked against themselves: the same games under two letters scored within 1 point. Replayed bot games matched exactly.
