# Cults: a critic's review

Claude, Nov 2, 2026, against the game branch (live v0.99.504). Sources: the rulebook, every live space and member, your playtest notes since Oct 22, 12 expert AI games with their ratings and notes, and 422 four-player Hard bot games (I counted the score and member numbers below from those games myself). I didn't sit a human table, so where I say how something feels, it's from your notes and the AI players' notes, not from my own play.

## The short verdict

Cults has some of the best pieces I've seen in a homemade worker placement game, and it doesn't yet have a spine. The wheel, the contests and four or five spaces are genuinely good. What's missing is one pressure that every color and every turn answers to. Without it, each turn is "take the biggest number on the board", which is exactly what you said after the Oct 27 playtest: "it doesn't feel like you can really cobble together a strategy." Eight colors each have their own little game (swap, copy, steal, skip, give, recolor, shop, cast out), and they sit side by side on the wheel instead of pulling on each other. That's why it has great parts and doesn't click.

## What works

**The wheel.** Rings that open by round (outer in round I, middle in II, center in III) and stay open is a better version of what Agricola does with its round cards: the board grows, the early spaces stay live, and everyone can see what's coming. A round III turn choosing among six spaces per color is a real draft. Keep it exactly as it is.

**Hold or spend.** Every contest is "most of this color held at round end" (+4, +6, +10). That makes every resource pull two ways: spend it on members and followers, or sit on it to win the rim. 33 of 42 AI players called this the heart of the game, and it's the one idea that runs through every color. It's also where most of the close games come from: 6 of 12 expert games were decided by 12 or less.

**A handful of spaces that read the table.**
- **Echo** ("Copy the previous action. +1 green.") is the best round I space in the game. Simple, and it gets better as the table takes better spaces. Rated 4.4 for strategy.
- **Promote** ("Give one of your members to a rival or the shop, and recruit one of theirs costing up to 2 more. +3 red.") and **Demote** (the same, "costing up to 2 less") are the best interaction in the game: they change what people own without just taking. Players named them among their best decisions in 4 of 5 Red games.
- **Crime Wave** ("Steal half of one rival's pile of one color, rounded down. +1 magenta.") is the check on hoarding that makes hold-or-spend honest. Players took it just so nobody could halve them, which is blocking at its best.
- **Last Call** ("+1 purple. +1 follower and +1 purple per minion you have left. Take the Goes Last token and put your other minions on it.") is the top-rated space (4.6). Trading tempo for position is a real worker placement idea.

**The snake with catch-up order.** Fewest followers goes first, and the ends get two turns in a row. That's a fair, quiet comeback rule that doesn't need a card.

**Cloaks.** Picking a cloak before round I, from last seat up, is a good opening decision and gives each player a direction from turn one.

## What doesn't cohere

### 1. One exchange rate flattens every decision
Any resource buys a follower 1 for 1, any time you have a recruit. Members' costs are about half "any". Yellow and Silver turn colors into other colors. So every space, steal and member reduces to one number in your head, and the right turn is whichever is biggest.

The best games in this genre all have a resource that isn't worth the same to everyone. Lords of Waterdeep quests need a specific mix of cubes, so a white cube is worth a lot to one player and nothing to another. Agricola makes you feed your family, so food has a deadline. Viticulture makes you turn grapes into wine into orders, so a space's value depends on what you already have. In Cults, a space pays the same to whoever takes it, and nothing you own makes it worth more to you than to a rival. That's the root of "biggest value turn".

### 2. Nothing presses on you
There's no must. If you skip a color, ignore a contest or never buy a member, nothing bad happens; you just score a little less. Worker placement games get their tension from a squeeze (food in Agricola, the harvest in Stone Age, the wine orders in Viticulture). Cults' only squeeze is the contest, and you can opt out of any contest at no cost. So the game is pleasant optimizing rather than tense planning.

### 3. Leaning into a color isn't rewarded
In the 422 Hard games, a cult ends with about 7 member cards across 3 colors, and its biggest color is 40% of its cards. Winners: 41%. Going deep in one color wins no more often than spreading out. The cloak points you somewhere on turn one, then the shop turns over before your turn comes back and you take whatever's best on the row.

That's a big part of why it doesn't feel like "leading a cult". Your cult has no shape. In Everdell or Terraforming Mars you can say "I'm the Critters player" or "I'm going plants"; in Cults you'd say "I bought some stuff."

### 4. All eight contests are the same contest
"Most of this color held" is a great rule, but every rim prints it. So the contests can't be what ties colors together, and they can't give a color its own reason to want other colors. The colors meet on the wheel, but they don't need each other.

### 5. Eight mini-games and a pile of exceptions
Each color is its own system: Red moves members, Green copies, Magenta steals, Purple bends turn order, Silver gives, Yellow recolors, Blue works the shop, White casts out. Each system needs rules for how it meets the others, and the rulebook shows it: copying a copy, what counts as recruiting, what a trade is versus a steal, Loyalists versus swaps, Impostors versus Doppelganger, skips that reach into a round with no turns left. On top of that the table has cloaks, the Goes Last token, the swap card, two deck sizes, round decks and a piles mode.

None of these is wrong on its own. Together they mean a new player spends their first game learning exceptions, and an experienced player still meets combinations nobody planned (Largesse paid 10 four times in one round through Echo, Reprise and Party Crasher). Most of the fixes so far have added a rule or a card to answer the last problem, which makes the next combination more likely.

### 6. Copies and joins repeat whatever is biggest
The copy verb is good. What it copies isn't controlled. All 6 expert games with Green named copying a top problem, each time aimed at a different big flat payout: **Con Men** ("Counts as 2 members. On Recruit: steal 5 resources.") through **Encore** ("Copy the On Recruit of a rival's member. +1 green."), **Largesse** ("Give rivals up to 10 silver from the bank, split as you choose. +1 follower per silver you give.") through Echo and **Party Crasher** ("On Use, once per game: your next minion can be placed on a space a rival already took."). This is the same root as point 1: when everything is a flat number, a copy just doubles the biggest one.

### 7. Round III swallows the game
Five minions, the center spaces, the biggest contests (+10) and the biggest flat payouts all land in one round. Half of all contest followers are won in round III. In 5 of 12 expert games, the winner's deciding swing came in their last few placements, from a card that acts after everyone or onto a taken space. A big last round is right for the genre; a last round where the build from rounds I and II matters less than the last two placements isn't.

### 8. Take-that without a shared answer
A lot of the interaction is taking: steal, swap, copy, skip. That's fine in a 2-player game. At 4 players, protection (**Lie Low**: "Steal 1. Rivals can't steal from you this round. +1 magenta."; **Bodyguards**: "Rivals can't steal from you.") pushes all the theft onto whoever didn't protect. In g11 one player lost 22 silver before their second round III turn, then two members. In the AI games, 30 decisive plays had 5 answered, 12 missed, 3 too costly to answer and 10 with no answer anywhere in the game. You already named this ("this just needs counterplay").

### 9. Purple fights the snake
The snake already decides turn order, and Purple's spaces that move your turns are the lowest rated in the game: **Crunch Time** ("+2 purple. Your next two turns come right after this one.") 1.7, **Right Away** ("+1 purple (+2 in round II, +3 in round III). Your next turn comes right after this one.") 1.9. Two turns in a row is already what the ends of the snake get for free. Purple works when it's about going last (Last Call), not when it's about going twice.

### 10. The game is moving faster than people play it
Since Oct 22 the table has gained cloaks, the Goes Last token, a swap card, a piles mode, two decks and dozens of price and text changes, mostly between human games. Most of the evidence is bots and AI players. That's fine for finding broken combos; it can't tell you whether the game clicks. A design that changes every evening never gets the five or six same-rules games a group needs before it can tell you what the game is.

## The changes most likely to make it click

These are directions for you to pick from, not cards. The first is the biggest lever and touches the economy, which is your call.

**1. Make a resource worth more to one player than another.** The single change with the most reach. Options:
- Members' costs lean much harder on their own color (most of the cost colored, little "any"), so what you hold decides what you can buy, and a color you aren't building is worth less to you.
- Follower buys at worse than 1 for 1, or in your cloak's color only at par, so members and spaces beat cashing in.
- Fewer ways to turn any color into any other.
A player would feel it at once: "I need two more red" instead of "what's the biggest number?"

**2. Reward going deep.** Your Oct 27 idea, set bonuses with members, is the right shape. Or each color's centerpiece member that scores per member of its color (**Mob**, "Game End: +1 follower per red member you have.", does this; there are only a few). The goal is that 5 members of one color plus 2 others beats 7 assorted members, and a table can see who's building what.

**3. Give each rim its own contest.** Keep "most held" as the base, and let each color's rim count something that's that color's own idea (most members you swapped in, most copies with your minion, most gifts given, most skipped turns). Then the contests point each player at a strategy and make colors want each other, instead of all asking the same question.

**4. Make round III pay for what you built.** Already on your Oct 27 list and not built: one center space per color that pays by your members or your rounds I and II, not by your pile. That fixes the last-placement swing more cleanly than more limits on Last Call and Party Crasher.

**5. Freeze one table and play it.** Pick one setup (Classic, the 10-card deck, cloaks on, piles off, swap card on or off), stop changing rules between games, and play it 4 or 5 times with the same friends. The changes above are big; they need a stable base to land on, and you need games on one ruleset to know if they worked.

Things I'd park rather than fix: Purple's go-twice spaces (rework toward going last), and the swap card and piles mode until the core clicks, since each adds a system.
